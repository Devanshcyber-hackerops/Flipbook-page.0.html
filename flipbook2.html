Swastik Ahaar Category

Fruits
  • Definition and examples: Fruits are edible plant products that commonly provide water, natural sugars, fibre, vitamins, minerals and bioactive compounds.
  • Nutritional contribution: Examples include guava, amla, citrus, banana, papaya, mango, apple, pomegranate, watermelon and seasonal local fruits. Whole fruit retains fibre and requires chewing; juice usually provides less fibre and can deliver sugars quickly.
  • Practical use: Choose whole fruit in varied colours and seasons. Pair fruit with meals or snacks according to appetite and individual needs. Frozen fruit without added sugar can also be nutritious.
  • Limitations and cautions: Fruit is not unlimited or a treatment for disease. People who need to manage carbohydrate intake can still often include fruit with suitable portions and professional guidance. Juice, even without added sugar, should not routinely replace whole fruit.

Vegetables and green leafy vegetables
  • Definition and examples: Vegetables include edible leaves, stems, flowers, fruits and other plant parts. Green leafy vegetables include spinach, amaranth leaves, fenugreek leaves, mustard greens and regional leafy varieties.
  • Nutritional contribution: Vegetables contribute fibre, water, potassium, folate, vitamin C, carotenoids and many phytochemicals. Dark green and orange vegetables often provide carotenoids; vitamin C content can be reduced by prolonged heating and storage.
  • Practical use: Include cooked sabzi, leafy saag, mixed vegetable curry, salads and vegetables in dal, poha or khichdi. Wash under clean running water and avoid cross-contamination.
  • Limitations and cautions: Nutrient content varies by species, freshness and cooking. Excessive washing after cutting and long boiling can cause nutrient losses. Raw produce may be unsuitable for some people with weakened immunity unless handled safely.

Grains and cereals
  • Definition and examples: Cereals are the edible seeds of grasses. Indian examples include rice, wheat, maize, barley, oats and millets such as jowar, bajra, ragi, foxtail millet and little millet
  • Nutritional contribution: They mainly supply carbohydrate for energy. Whole and less-refined grains also provide dietary fibre, B vitamins and minerals; millets differ in their protein, fibre and mineral composition. Ragi is notable for calcium relative to many other cereals, but actual intake and absorption depend on portion and preparation.
  • Practical use: Use rice, roti, bhakri, dalia, oats, millet khichdi or fermented grain batters. Prefer a mix of whole and refined grains according to access, tolerance and nutritional needs.
  • Limitations and cautions: Refining removes bran and germ, reducing fibre and some micronutrients. Whole grains may improve fibre intake but can be unsuitable in certain gastrointestinal conditions or for people with specific intolerances. Gluten-containing wheat, barley and rye must be avoided by people with coeliac disease.

Pulses and legumes
  • Definition and examples: Pulses are the dried edible seeds of leguminous plants, including lentils, chickpeas, pigeon pea, mung bean, urad, peas, cowpea and kidney beans.
  • Nutritional contribution: They provide plant protein, carbohydrate, fibre, folate, iron, potassium and other minerals. Their amino-acid profile complements cereal proteins; combining varied plant protein sources across the day can help meet essential amino-acid needs.
  • Practical use: Examples include dal, chana, rajma, usal, sprouts, sambar, besan chilla and hummus. Soaking and thorough cooking improve palatability and reduce some antinutritional factors.
  • Limitations and cautions: Some pulses cause gas because fermentable carbohydrates are digested by gut microbes. Increase fibre gradually and cook beans properly. Raw or undercooked kidney beans can contain harmful lectins; follow safe soaking and boiling instructions.

Roots and tubers
  • Definition and examples: Roots and tubers are underground plant parts that store energy. Examples include potato, sweet potato, yam, colocasia, tapioca, carrot, beetroot and radish.
  • Nutritional contribution: Potato, yam and tapioca are mainly starch sources. Sweet potato also supplies beta-carotene in orange varieties. Carrot supplies carotenoids; beetroot provides folate and betalain pigments. The nutritional contribution depends on variety and cooking.
  • Practical use: Use boiled, roasted or lightly cooked tubers in meals; combine starchy tubers with pulses and vegetables rather than treating them as the only component.
  • Limitations and cautions: Deep-frying greatly increases energy density. Tapioca/cassava requires appropriate processing and cooking because improperly prepared cassava can contain cyanogenic compounds. Do not eat unknown wild tubers.

Dairy foods and alternatives
  • Definition and examples: Dairy foods include milk, curd/yoghurt, buttermilk, paneer and cheese. Alternatives include fortified soy or other plant beverages, depending on their protein and micronutrient content.
  • Nutritional contribution: Milk and curd can provide protein, calcium, vitamin B12 and riboflavin; amounts vary by product and fortification. Paneer supplies protein and fat. Fortified soy beverages may provide protein and calcium if the label confirms fortification.
  • Practical use: Use milk, curd, chaas or paneer if tolerated and desired. For dairy-free diets, choose fortified unsweetened alternatives and ensure adequate protein, calcium, vitamin B12 and vitamin D from appropriate sources.
  • Limitations and cautions: Lactose intolerance differs from milk allergy. Plant beverages are not nutritionally interchangeable: many almond, rice or oat drinks contain little protein. Check labels for protein, calcium, vitamin D, B12 and added sugar.

Nuts and seeds
  • Definition and examples: Nuts and oilseeds include groundnuts, almonds, walnuts, cashews, sesame, flaxseed, chia, pumpkin and sunflower seeds.
  • Nutritional contribution: They supply unsaturated fats, energy, protein, fibre, vitamin E, magnesium and other minerals. Flax and chia provide alpha-linolenic acid, a plant omega-3 fatty acid. Sesame contributes calcium, though absorption varies.
  • Practical use: Add modest portions to breakfast, chutneys, salads, snacks or vegetable dishes. Choose unsalted varieties more often and store them in cool, dry conditions.
  • Limitations and cautions: Nuts are energy-dense and can trigger serious allergies. They are choking hazards for young children in whole form. Salted, sugar-coated or heavily fried versions can add excess sodium, sugar or fat.

Oils and fats
  • Definition and examples: Dietary fats include visible cooking oils, ghee, butter, spreads and fats naturally present in foods.
  • Nutritional contribution: Fats provide concentrated energy, essential fatty acids, support cell membranes and help absorb vitamins A, D, E and K. Unsaturated fats are generally preferable to trans fats and excessive saturated fats.
  • Practical use: Use a measured amount of cooking oil and vary sources where practical, such as groundnut, mustard, sesame, sunflower, soybean or other locally available oils. Include nuts and seeds as food sources of fat.
  • Limitations and cautions: All oils are energy-dense. Repeatedly heating oil, deep-frying often and consuming high amounts of saturated or trans fats can worsen dietary quality. Ghee is not a medicine and should be used in moderation.

Herbs and spices
  • Definition and examples: Herbs are leafy or tender plant parts used for flavour; spices may come from seeds, bark, roots, fruits or flower buds. Examples include coriander, cumin, turmeric, ginger, garlic, chilli, pepper, cinnamon and cloves.
  • Nutritional contribution: Spices can contribute flavour and small amounts of micronutrients and phytochemicals. Their typical serving sizes are small, so they should not be counted as the main source of nutrients or treated as medicines.
  • Practical use: Use spices to create flavour and reduce dependence on excess salt. Store dry spices away from heat and moisture; buy from reliable sources.
  • Limitations and cautions: Laboratory antioxidant activity does not automatically prove a clinical health benefit at normal food doses. Concentrated extracts and supplements can interact with medicines or cause adverse effects.

Traditional Indian mixed dishes
  • Definition and examples: Mixed dishes combine several ingredients and cooking methods. Examples include khichdi, idli-sambar, dal-rice, rajma-chawal, thali, vegetable pulao, dosa, dhokla, millet bhakri with pithla and curd-rice.
  • Nutritional contribution: Their nutritional value depends on the proportions of grain, pulse, vegetables, dairy or alternatives, oil, salt and serving size. A mixed dish can be balanced, but its name alone does not guarantee nutritional adequacy.
  • Practical use: Build a meal by adding a pulse or protein source, vegetables, a grain or tuber and a suitable fruit or dairy/fortified alternative. Modify recipes to reduce excess salt and oil while retaining taste.
  • Limitations and cautions: Some versions may be high in refined starch, salt, oil or added sugar. Portion size and accompaniments matter. Traditional foods should be assessed by ingredients and preparation, not by reputation alone.
